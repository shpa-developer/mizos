How to Share Measurements with Your Tailor

Updated November 2026 · 6 min read

You've taken your measurements carefully. You know your chest is 40 inches and your inseam is 30. Now you need to get those numbers to your tailor — and this is where things often go wrong.

A measurement that's taken perfectly but communicated poorly is as bad as a wrong measurement. This guide covers what information your tailor actually needs, the best ways to share it, and how to avoid the miscommunication that leads to ill-fitting clothes.

What Information Does Your Tailor Need?

Beyond the raw numbers, your tailor needs context. Here's the complete picture:

  • The measurements themselves — clearly labelled with what each number represents.
  • The unit — inches or centimetres. Never assume your tailor will guess correctly.
  • Who the measurements are for — name of the person, especially if you're ordering for family members.
  • The garment type — "shirt measurements" vs "suit measurements" tells the tailor which numbers to pay attention to.
  • Fit preference — slim fit, regular, relaxed. This changes how the tailor interprets your numbers.
  • Any special notes — "left shoulder sits slightly higher," "prefers extra room in the thigh," etc.

Common mistake: Sending a list of unlabelled numbers like "40, 34, 30, 16.5" over WhatsApp. Your tailor has dozens of customers. Without labels, they have to guess which number is which — and guesses lead to mistakes.

Traditional Ways to Share Measurements

In person

The gold standard. You visit the tailor, they measure you themselves with their own tape, and they record the numbers in their register. There's no chance of miscommunication because the tailor controls the entire process.

The problem: It requires a physical visit, which isn't always possible — especially for repeat orders, family members in different cities, or when ordering from an out-of-town tailor you found online.

Phone call

You read numbers out loud while the tailor writes them down. Fast, but error-prone. "Sixteen" and "sixty" sound similar. "Thirty" and "thirteen" get confused. Background noise makes it worse. And there's no written record for either of you to verify later.

Written note or photo

You write measurements on paper and photograph them, or text a list. Better than a phone call because there's a record, but handwriting can be hard to read, and texts tend to be poorly formatted — a wall of numbers with no clear labels.

The Digital Alternative

The problem with all traditional methods is that the measurement data isn't structured. It's either spoken, scrawled, or typed as free text. That means someone has to interpret it, and interpretation is where errors creep in.

A better approach is to send measurements in a structured, labelled format that leaves no room for misreading. Here's what that looks like in practice:

Typical WhatsApp message

  • "bhai shirt ke liye
  • 40 chest
  • 16.5 neck
  • sleeve 25
  • length 30"

Structured sharing

  • Rahul — Shirt
  • Chest: 40 in
  • Neck: 16.5 in
  • Sleeve: 25 in
  • Length: 30 in

The second version takes no more effort to send, but it's dramatically easier for the tailor to read, verify, and use. Every number is labelled. The unit is clear. The person and garment type are identified.

Tips for Clear Communication

Regardless of how you share, these practices prevent the most common miscommunication:

Always label every measurement. "Chest: 40 in" is unambiguous. "40" by itself is not. Even if you think it's obvious from context, label it anyway. Your tailor handles many customers and shouldn't have to guess.

Specify the unit every time. Some tailors work in inches, others in centimetres, and some use both depending on the garment. Don't assume. An unlabelled "16.5" could be 16.5 inches (a neck measurement) or 16.5 cm (completely different context).

Include the person's name. If you're sharing measurements for your spouse, your child, and yourself in the same conversation, label whose measurements are whose. Confusion here means someone gets the wrong garment.

Mention when the measurements were taken. If your measurements are from six months ago and you've had a significant weight change, say so. Better yet, re-measure before ordering. Our guide to measuring yourself walks you through the process.

Confirm receipt. Ask your tailor to read back the measurements or confirm the key numbers. A 30-second check prevents a weeks-long tailoring mistake.

Keep a copy. Whatever you send, keep a record. If there's a dispute about what was communicated, having your own copy is essential. This is another advantage of using an app — your measurements are saved on your phone and the shared message exists in your chat history.

For family orders: If you're ordering for multiple family members (common for weddings and festivals), share each person's measurements as a separate message rather than cramming everything into one. It's easier for the tailor to file and reference.

What About Privacy?

Body measurements are personal information. Before sharing them through any channel, consider who else might see them. A WhatsApp message to your tailor is between you and them — that's fine. Posting measurements publicly or sharing them with unverified online services is a different matter.

The Bottom Line

Good measurement sharing is about clarity: label everything, specify units, name the person, and keep a record. Whether you do it with pen and paper, a typed message, or a dedicated app, the goal is the same — make sure your tailor gets exactly the numbers you intended, with zero room for misinterpretation.

The 30 seconds you spend formatting your measurements clearly will save you days of waiting for a garment that needs to be redone.
